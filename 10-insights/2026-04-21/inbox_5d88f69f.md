## Stop Burning Your AI Credits on the Wrong Model — Here’s What I Use Instead

開發者分享一套三層模型路由策略，以解決 AI token 成本失控問題（預算從週三耗盡改善至月內充足）。核心原則是將任務分離為「思考階段」（用 GPT-4o mini 規劃）和「執行階段」（用 Sonnet 實現）。規劃文件需明確列出接受條件、應做與不應做事項、約束條件；接著用 Sonnet 或 Opus 驗證計畫（僅用於稽核，不重做）；最後按 acceptance criteria 逐項實現。以 SSO 整合（OpenID Connect PKCE flow、HttpOnly Secure cookie）為實例，展示規劃階段識別五項關鍵不確定項，避免後續除錯成本。這套方法將高級模型的使用從 15 次請求降至 2–3 次，成本大幅下降且品質提升。

### 重點
- 三層策略：小模型規劃（快速、低成本）→ Sonnet/Opus 稽核（驗證而非重建）→ 按 criteria 逐步實現
- 規劃文件是 contract：必須列出 acceptance criteria、what/what-not-to-do、constraints，逼迫前期思考
- Sonnet 用於大多數實現；Opus 只用於高複雜度（安全邏輯、架構決策）— 根據複雜度選模型，而非盲目用最強模型

# Stop Burning Your AI Credits on the Wrong Model — Here’s What I Use Instead

<p><em>How I use cheap models to plan, powerful models to verify, and the right model to build — and why this is the only workflow that actually scales.</em></p><p>My premium AI credits used to vanish by Wednesday.</p><p>Not because I was doing anything wrong. I was doing everything “right” — opening the most powerful model available, describing the task, and letting it think through everything at once. Planning, architecture, implementation — one long session. It felt productive. It looked impressive.</p><p>Then Thursday would arrive, and I’d be staring at a rate limit for the rest of the week.</p><p>That’s not a workflow. That’s expensive guesswork with good autocomplete.</p><p>Over the past few months I’ve completely rethought how I use AI models — not just <em>which</em> model, but <em>when</em>, <em>why</em>, and <em>what I actually ask it to do</em>. The result is a tiered system that stretches my 100 monthly premium requests across the entire month, ships cleaner code, and catches problems I used to find in production.</p><p>Here’s exactly how it works — including the messy, real authentication integration that proved to me this approach isn’t just theory.</p><h3>The Core Insight Most Engineers Miss</h3><p>Every task you give an AI model has a <em>thinking phase</em> and an <em>execution phase</em>.</p><p>The thinking phase is: What exactly needs to happen? What are the constraints? What does done look like? What should we absolutely <em>not</em> do? Where are the hidden traps?</p><p>The execution phase is: Actually build it.</p><p>Most engineers use their most expensive model for both. That’s where the waste is — because the thinking phase doesn’t require raw power. It requires structure. And smaller, cheaper models are remarkably capable at structure when you guide them correctly.</p><p>The expensive model’s value isn’t in generating the plan. It’s in auditing one.</p><h3>Step 1: Use a Small Model to Build the Plan</h3><p>When a task lands on my plate — a feature, a bug fix, a security integration, a refactor — my first move is <em>not</em> to open my top-tier model. It’s to open <strong>GPT-4o mini/GPT-5o-mini (any free model provided by company for unlimited use) </strong>and ask it to build me a full planning document.</p><p>Not a vague summary. A proper MD file with:</p><ul><li><strong>The Problem / Item</strong> — what we’re solving and why it matters</li><li><strong>Acceptance Criteria</strong> — exactly what done looks like, numbered and testable</li><li><strong>How to Fix It</strong> — the approach, step by step</li><li><strong>What Should Be Done</strong> — specific actions, in order</li><li><strong>What Should NOT Be Done</strong> — the guardrails, the anti-patterns, the things that look tempting but will cause pain</li><li><strong>Constraints</strong> — performance limits, dependency rules, compatibility requirements, non-negotiables</li></ul><p>This MD file becomes the contract for the entire task.</p><p>The prompt I use:</p><blockquote>“You are a senior engineer reviewing a task. Produce a structured planning document: problem statement, acceptance criteria, implementation approach, what to do, what NOT to do, and constraints. Be specific. Flag ambiguity instead of assuming. Do not write code yet.”</blockquote><p>Does the small model get everything right? No. That’s not the point. The point is it gets me 80% of the way there in 5% of the token cost — and forces me to think through the problem before spending a single premium credit.</p><h3>Step 2: Verify with a Powerful Model — But Only to Audit</h3><p>Once I have the plan and the MD file, I don’t just run with it. I bring in Claude Sonnet or Opus — but <strong>only</strong> for verification, not to redo the work.</p><p>I hand it the planning document and ask one focused question:</p><blockquote>“Review this plan. Is the approach sound? Are the acceptance criteria complete? Are there hidden risks, missing constraints, or incorrect assumptions? Fix anything that’s wrong.”</blockquote><p>This is a radically more efficient use of a powerful model. Instead of asking it to think from scratch, I’m asking it to audit. The input is already structured. The output is targeted corrections — not a rewrite.</p><p>I choose between Sonnet and Opus based on task complexity:</p><ul><li><strong>Sonnet</strong> — most features, bug fixes, integrations, refactors</li><li><strong>Opus</strong> — architecture decisions, security-critical flows, anything where the cost of a wrong plan is high</li></ul><p>By the time this step is done, I have a senior-reviewed, verified plan and I’ve spent 2–3 premium requests on planning instead of 15.</p><h3>Step 3: Implement One Acceptance Criterion at a Time</h3><p>Now comes execution — and this is where the second big shift happened.</p><p>I don’t hand the entire plan to a model and say “build it.” I work through the acceptance criteria <strong>one item at a time</strong>, using the verified plan as context for each call. Sonnet handles most implementation. I switch to Opus only when the problem genuinely needs it: subtle security logic, cross-cutting architectural changes, situations where a confident but wrong answer causes production damage.</p><p>For mechanical tasks — boilerplate, scaffolding, repetitive test cases — I stay on small models. The cost difference is significant and the quality ceiling is plenty high enough.</p><p>The workflow in full:</p><pre>Task arrives<br />  → Small model builds plan MD (cheap, fast, structured)<br />  → I review the plan — fill gaps, confirm constraints<br />  → Sonnet or Opus audits + corrects the plan (targeted, efficient)<br />  → I verify the final plan<br />  → Implement task-by-task with Sonnet (Opus for high-complexity steps)<br />  → Deliver</pre><h3>What This Actually Looks Like in Practice</h3><p>Let me give you a real example — not sanitized, not simplified.</p><p>I was handed an SSO integration task: wire an enterprise identity provider (OpenID Connect, PKCE flow) into a developer portal, covering both frontend and backend, with a secure cookie-based session, local dev setup, and an API gateway sitting in the middle.</p><p>On paper that’s “integrate auth.” In practice it’s a minefield.</p><h3>The Planning Phase</h3><p>I fed the task description to GPT-4o mini with my planning prompt. In about two minutes it produced a full MD planning document. The structure it generated covered the auth flow, the redirect URI setup, the token storage approach, and the local dev prerequisites.</p><p>Critically, it also flagged five things it wasn’t sure about — things I needed to confirm before implementation. That alone saved at least one debugging session.</p><p>Here’s a shortened version of what the acceptance criteria section looked like:</p><pre>## Acceptance Criteria<br />- [ ] SSO login flow completes end-to-end: user authenticates at identity provider,<br />      backend exchanges authorization code, session cookie is set.<br />- [ ] Access token is stored in HttpOnly Secure cookie — NOT in localStorage or URL params.<br />- [ ] Callback page uses router.replace() so back-button does not replay the callback.<br />- [ ] PKCE state/nonce storage survives backend restarts (no in-memory Map in production).<br />- [ ] All authenticated API calls use credentials: 'include' — no Authorization header<br />      required from the frontend.<br />- [ ] JWT signature is verified via JWKS — not just decoded.<br />- [ ] Route guard middleware validates session cookie, not a plain spoofable header.<br />## What Should NOT Be Done<br />- Do not return tokens in redirect URLs (e.g. ?token=...)<br />- Do not store access or refresh tokens in localStorage<br />- Do not trust plain x-user-id header without also verifying session<br />- Do not decode JWT without verifying the signature first<br />- Do not use an in-memory store for PKCE state in production</pre><p>A small model produced that. Not perfect — but solid enough to be the basis for a real audit.</p><h3>The Verification Phase — Where It Got Valuable</h3><p>I handed the plan to Sonnet and asked it to audit. This is where things got interesting.</p><p>It came back with <strong>12 specific issues</strong> in the existing codebase that the plan hadn’t accounted for — bugs already present in the running code that would silently break the integration or create security holes:</p><ul><li>The existing callback handler was appending the access token directly to the redirect URL as a query parameter (?token=...). Any browser history, proxy log, or referrer header would expose it.</li><li>The frontend hook was storing both access and refresh tokens in localStorage — XSS-accessible.</li><li>JWT verification was calling decodeJwt() without verifying the signature, then running a nonce check on an <em>unverified</em> token.</li><li>The route guard middleware was trusting a plain x-user-id header with no validation — trivially spoofable.</li><li>The redirect URI in the frontend config was missing the application’s base path prefix, meaning the identity provider would reject every authorization request before it even started.</li><li>The PKCE state store was an in-memory Map — guaranteed to fail on restart or in any multi-instance deployment.</li><li>The API fetch helper never sent credentials: 'include', so cookies were never attached to authenticated requests.</li></ul><p>None of these were in the original task description. None would have been caught by running the happy path locally. All of them were ship-blockers.</p><p>Sonnet didn’t just find them — it updated the MD file with exact file paths, line numbers, the nature of each bug, and the specific fix required. The planning document went from a reasonable draft to a verified, senior-level implementation spec.</p><p><strong>That single Sonnet call probably saved 2–3 days of debugging.</strong></p><h3>The Local Setup Failure (And Why the Plan Caught It)</h3><p>Here’s the part I don’t usually tell people.</p><p>The first time I tried to run the integration locally, it failed. Not because of a code bug — because of infrastructure I hadn’t set up.</p><p>The identity provider requires HTTPS for non-localhost redirect URIs. My backend was running on HTTP. The redirect was rejected before a single line of auth code ran.</p><p>The plan had documented this requirement under constraints:</p><pre>## Constraints<br />- Identity provider requires HTTPS for all non-localhost redirect URIs.<br />- Local domain must resolve to 127.0.0.1 (/etc/hosts entry required).<br />- A locally trusted TLS certificate is needed (mkcert recommended).<br />- A local reverse proxy (Caddy) must terminate TLS on port 4000 and forward to the backend process.<br />- Redis must be running for PKCE state storage (docker run redis:7-alpine).</pre><p>Because the constraint was documented, I knew exactly what to fix. I set up mkcert, wrote a two-line Caddyfile, added the local domain to /etc/hosts, started Redis in Docker, and the flow worked on the next attempt.</p><p>Without the plan, I would have been Googling symptoms — “identity provider redirect_uri mismatch”, “HTTPS required for OAuth callback”, “PKCE state not found” — and slowly reassembling the picture from scratch. That’s an afternoon gone.</p><h3>The Implementation Phase</h3><p>With the verified plan, implementation was methodical. I worked through the acceptance criteria one by one, using Sonnet for each:</p><ol><li>Replace in-memory PKCE state store with a Redis-backed TTL store</li><li>Add JWKS signature verification using jose — replace the unsafe decodeJwt() call</li><li>Update the callback handler to set HttpOnly; Secure; SameSite=None cookies — remove the token from the redirect URL</li><li>Update the frontend hook to use credentials: 'include' everywhere — remove localStorage token storage</li><li>Fix the redirect URI config to include the application base path prefix</li><li>Fix the route guard to validate the session cookie instead of trusting the plain header</li></ol><p>Each implementation call had the verified plan as context. Each one was scoped to one acceptance criterion. No ambiguity. No context bleeding between unrelated concerns. No rewriting something because the model forgot what we decided three steps ago.</p><p><strong>Total premium requests for the entire integration: 11.</strong> Planning and verification: 3. Implementation: 8. The integration shipped clean. The 12 bugs found during verification never made it to production.</p><h3>Why Most Engineers Won’t Do This</h3><p>The honest reason is that it feels slower at the start.</p><p>Writing a planning document before you write code is a discipline. Especially when you’re used to firing off a prompt and getting something that <em>looks</em> like progress.</p><p>But the plan isn’t overhead. It’s the work. Every ambiguity you resolve in the planning phase is a debugging session you never have to have. Every constraint you write down is a production incident you never have to respond to at 2am.</p><p>The second reason is that people confuse model power with model value. Opus isn’t valuable because it generates the plan. It’s valuable because it catches what the plan got wrong. Those are different jobs, and they have very different costs.</p><h3>The Numbers That Changed My Thinking</h3><p>Before this workflow: 100 premium requests gone in 2–3 days, mid-week rate limits, incomplete implementations, bugs found in review or production.</p><p>After: 30–40 premium requests per month on average. The rest available when I genuinely need them. Cleaner implementations. Bugs caught before the first commit.</p><p>The auth integration I described above — 12 security issues found and fixed before shipping — would have cost me 40–50 premium requests under the old approach. It cost 11. And it was more thorough.</p><h3>The Framework, Restated Simply</h3><p><strong>Small model</strong> → draft the plan (cheap, fast, structured)</p><p><strong>You</strong> → review the plan, fill gaps, confirm constraints</p><p><strong>Powerful model</strong> → audit the plan, find what’s wrong, fix it</p><p><strong>You</strong> → verify the final plan</p><p><strong>Right model for the job</strong> → implement one criterion at a time</p><p>That’s it. No secret prompt. No special tooling. Just a deliberate separation between planning work and execution work — and using the right tool for each.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*6fhECUi9BRTdGptDLFPK9g.png" /><figcaption>Created by Gemini using Prompt from user</figcaption></figure><h3>The Takeaway</h3><p>You don’t need to burn premium credits to ship great work. You need a system that respects what each model is actually good at.</p><p>The most expensive model you have is not a planning tool. It’s an auditor. Use it that way, and it’ll catch things that would have cost you days. Use it for everything, and you’ll run out of credits before you run out of tasks.</p><p>That’s not a limitation. That’s the job.</p><p><em>I write about building real systems with AI — not just talking about building them. If this changed how you think about your workflow, follow along. Try this out and tell me how you felt and it worked for you too or missing something.</em></p><img alt="" height="1" src="https://medium.com/_/stat?event=post.clientViewed&amp;referrerSource=full_rss&amp;postId=b5a66024f847" width="1" /><hr /><p><a href="https://blog.stackademic.com/stop-using-your-best-ai-model-for-everything-its-making-your-work-worse-b5a66024f847">Stop Burning Your AI Credits on the Wrong Model — Here’s What I Use Instead</a> was originally published in <a href="https://blog.stackademic.com">Stackademic</a> on Medium, where people are continuing the conversation by highlighting and responding to this story.</p>
