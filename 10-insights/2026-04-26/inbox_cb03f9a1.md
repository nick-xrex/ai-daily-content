<h4><strong>Quantum Physics · Solar Energy · The Exciton Breakthrough</strong></h4><p>Scientists discovered a new quantum state at the interface between two materials that transfers energy in one ten-trillionth of a second. A second team broke the absolute limit of solar efficiency 130% quantum yield from a single photon. The physics of solar power is being rewritten, in two separate labs, in the same season.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*e2eaKc7OxU859iqdemYe4g.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*wAcnK2pE_rO5mtf1k-MWtQ.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*bAC038SXAL8CM2CH9YbJkw.png" /></figure><p>T amil Nadu gets around 300 sunny days a year. Puducherry, where I study IT, sits on the Bay of Bengal coast with some of the highest solar irradiance in India. Every time I look at the solar panels on the institute rooftop, I think about the same number: 33%. That is the approximate maximum efficiency of a conventional silicon solar cell the Shockley-Queisser limit, a theoretical ceiling derived from the physics of photon absorption. For six decades, every solar engineer on Earth has been working inside that constraint. Two papers published in the past six months suggest the constraint itself may be breakable.</p><p>The first breakthrough came in October 2025, published in Nature Physics by researchers from the Universities of Göttingen, Marburg, Humboldt Berlin, and Graz. They discovered a new quantum state hybrid excitons at the interface between two types of semiconductor materials, and demonstrated energy transfer across that interface in one ten-trillionth of a second. The second came in March 2026, published in the Journal of the American Chemical Society by Kyushu University and Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz: a demonstration of 130% quantum yield from a single photon, using a process called singlet fission that generates two excitons from one photon. Both breakthroughs involve the same quantum particle the exciton and both point toward the same destination: solar cells that capture and convert sunlight far more efficiently than anything that exists today.</p><p>Let me explain what an exciton is, why it matters, and what these two results actually mean for the future of solar energy.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/932/1*p27CsIwoequ8gRT65lzbWw.png" /></figure><p>When light strikes a solar cell, it doesn’t directly produce electricity. What happens first is more interesting: a photon a particle of light is absorbed by the material, and its energy excites an electron out of its ground state into a higher energy level, leaving behind a positively charged “hole” where the electron was. The electron and the hole don’t immediately separate. They are attracted to each other by electrical force and form a bound pair — an electron-hole pair held together by quantum mechanical interaction. This bound pair is called an exciton.</p><p>The exciton is not a real particle in the way an electron is. It is a quasiparticle a concept that describes the collective behaviour of the electron and hole as they move through the material together. But it behaves like a particle in the sense that it has defined properties: energy, momentum, spin, and a characteristic lifetime before it either dissociates into free charges (which produce current) or recombines and releases the energy as heat or light (which wastes it). The efficiency of a solar cell depends critically on what happens to excitons after they form. If they dissociate quickly into useful current, efficiency is high. If they recombine or lose energy before dissociating, efficiency is low.</p><p>Different materials produce different types of excitons with different properties. In organic semiconductors carbon-based materials used in flexible electronics and OLEDs excitons are tightly bound, called Frenkel excitons. They are easy to form but hard to split into useful current. In two-dimensional inorganic semiconductors atomically thin materials like molybdenum disulphide or tungsten diselenide excitons are more loosely bound, called Wannier-Mott excitons. They are mobile and easier to convert to current, but harder to manufacture at scale. The question that drove the Göttingen team’s research: what happens at the interface between these two types of material?</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*TpA7tDyt2hRCp-DmiVlBQA.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/930/1*LdKca5J-oVvADiMNLDAOzQ.png" /></figure><p>The Göttingen team, led by Prof. Stefan Mathias and first author Wiebke Bennecke, combined two specific materials: WSe2 tungsten diselenide, a two-dimensional semiconductor and PTCDA perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic dianhydride, an organic semiconductor. They stacked these two materials with atomic precision and illuminated them with light, then used a technique called momentum microscopy to watch what happened at the interface at ultrafast time resolution one quadrillionth of a second.</p><p>What they observed was a new type of exciton that had not been directly measured before. When a photon was absorbed by the WSe2 layer, an exciton formed but instead of staying within the 2D material as a standard Wannier-Mott exciton, it hybridised across the interface. The electron remained in the 2D material (mobile, easy to extract) while the hole spread into the organic PTCDA layer (flexible, tuneable). The result was a Hybrid Frenkel-Wannier exciton: combining the electron mobility of the 2D semiconductor with the adaptability of the organic material.</p><p>The critical finding: energy transfer across the interface mediated by these hybrid excitons happened in less than one ten-trillionth (10⁻¹³) of a second. To appreciate this timescale a femtosecond is 10⁻¹⁵ seconds, and the fastest chemical reactions happen on femtosecond timescales. The hybrid exciton energy transfer at 10⁻¹³ seconds is extraordinarily rapid by any measure. This speed matters enormously for solar cells: energy that transfers quickly becomes useful current before it can be lost as heat. Energy that transfers slowly is energy wasted.</p><p>“The key to this ultrafast energy transfer is the formation of hybrid excitons, for which we have now found a tell-tale experimental signature,” explains Prof. Mathias. “Our results allow us to better understand and efficiently harness the fundamental processes behind energy and charge transfer in semiconductor nanostructures. This is a crucial step toward the development of efficient solar cells, ultrafast optoelectronic components, and novel applications in quantum technology.”</p><p>The result was published in Nature Physics one of the most rigorous physics journals in October 2025, with the full author list spanning four universities across two countries. The experimental evidence was confirmed by theoretical calculations using many-body perturbation theory. This is not a preliminary result. It is a fully characterised quantum mechanical phenomenon with a clear experimental signature and a validated theoretical framework.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/919/1*odmMVHO8pbV1-85_g5lO3Q.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/929/1*P5fcl8zGv_njnRcGFL5dgw.png" /></figure><p>The second breakthrough attacks the same problem from a different angle. The Shockley-Queisser limit approximately 33% maximum efficiency for a single-junction silicon solar cell arises because photons with more energy than the semiconductor’s bandgap lose their excess energy as heat. High-energy photons waste their surplus. Low-energy photons aren’t absorbed at all. The 33% ceiling is the theoretical consequence of this fundamental inefficiency in how silicon interacts with the solar spectrum.</p><p>Kyushu University’s team, led by Prof. Yoichi Sasaki, exploited a quantum phenomenon called singlet fission to break this ceiling. In singlet fission, a single high-energy spin-singlet exciton produced when one photon is absorbed splits spontaneously into two lower-energy spin-triplet excitons. One photon in, two excitons out. If those two excitons can both be converted to electricity, the quantum yield exceeds 100%. In principle, singlet fission could push solar cell efficiency well beyond the Shockley-Queisser limit by ensuring no photon energy is wasted as heat.</p><p>The practical problem: the two triplet excitons produced by singlet fission are hard to capture before they recombine and lose their energy. A process called Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) tends to steal the energy before the multiplication can be harvested. Sasaki’s team solved this using a molybdenum-based metal complex a “spin-flip emitter” that selectively captures the multiplied triplet excitons after fission. The molybdenum complex locks in the energy and then converts it to useful emission through a quantum spin-flip process. The result: 1.3 molybdenum-based metal complexes excited per photon absorbed a quantum yield of 130%.</p><p>This is still at proof-of-concept stage: the experiment was conducted in liquid solution, not in a solid-state solar panel. Converting the demonstration to a manufacturable solar cell technology is a significant engineering challenge. But the physics is demonstrated. One photon can produce more than one usable energy quantum. The ceiling that solar engineers have been working beneath for sixty years has a hole in it.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*ElhCFqesqFGOJL2W2NQWAQ.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/928/1*Sgq6ftx8iAjjrCVW1XE7Yg.png" /></figure><p>Read individually, each result is significant. Read together, they sketch the outline of a completely new generation of solar technology that addresses the solar efficiency problem from two complementary directions simultaneously.</p><p>The hybrid exciton result addresses energy transfer speed the bottleneck that determines how much of the energy absorbed by a solar cell actually reaches the electrodes as usable current before being lost. The ultrafast 10⁻¹³-second transfer enabled by hybrid excitons at 2D-organic interfaces means that energy deposited anywhere in the material reaches where it needs to go before it can dissipate. Less waste. Higher efficiency.</p><p>The singlet fission result addresses photon utilisation the ceiling that determines how much of the energy in each photon can be extracted. By generating two excitons from one high-energy photon, singlet fission allows photons that would otherwise waste their surplus energy as heat to contribute their full energy content to electrical current. One photon, two units of work. The ceiling itself moves upward.</p><p>A solar cell that combines ultrafast exciton transfer with singlet fission energy multiplication would theoretically operate above the Shockley-Queisser limit while also minimising internal losses. Neither breakthrough is close to becoming a commercial product the Göttingen result is fundamental physics, and the Kyushu result is proof-of-concept in solution. But the direction is clear, and the physics is real and peer-reviewed. The constraints that solar engineers have accepted as permanent since 1961 are now two demonstrated breakthroughs closer to being overcome.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*_RhctYZihDaV0yG0qFiqUw.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/933/1*2WzV3bMjf4IbkGK4rWPxWQ.png" /></figure><p>I do not study solar engineering. I study networks and systems, and the connection between quantum physics and my coursework is not obvious until you think about what both fields are fundamentally about: energy, information, and the efficiency of their transfer. The same question that makes quantum repeaters important for networking how do you move information from one place to another without losing it? is the question that makes hybrid excitons important for solar cells: how do you move energy from where it’s generated to where it’s needed without losing it to recombination and heat?</p><p>India installed roughly 20 gigawatts of solar capacity in 2024 the most in a single year in the country’s history. The government’s target is 500 gigawatts of renewable energy by 2030, with solar as the primary driver. Tamil Nadu alone has 8+ gigawatts of installed solar. If the Shockley-Queisser limit is a ceiling at 33% and we are typically achieving 20–22% in commercial panels, the gap between current technology and the theoretical maximum represents hundreds of gigawatts of potential capacity that the sun is delivering and our panels are wasting as heat.</p><p>Breakthroughs in exciton physics do not translate to rooftop panels in five years. The path from a Nature Physics result to a commercially manufactured solar cell takes a decade of materials engineering, fabrication development, stability testing, and cost reduction. The Kyushu result is still in liquid solution. The Göttingen result is fundamental science, not an engineering demonstration. I understand this.</p><p>But I also understand that the panels on my institute’s rooftop are built on physics that was fundamental science sixty years ago. The path from discovery to deployment is long and the steps are not guaranteed. What the hybrid exciton and singlet fission breakthroughs establish is that the path exists that the physics of solar energy has not reached its final form, that the 33% ceiling is a constraint of current materials rather than a law of nature, and that the quantum world contains mechanisms for harvesting sunlight that we are only beginning to understand. Three hundred sunny days a year in Puducherry. The sun is not the bottleneck. The physics is. And the physics is changing.</p><p><em>Sources → Bennecke et al., “Hybrid Frenkel–Wannier excitons facilitate ultrafast energy transfer at a 2D–organic interface,” Nature Physics, October 2025 (DOI: 10.1038/s41567–025–03075–5) · Sifuentes-Samanamud et al., “Exploring Spin-State Selective Harvesting Pathways from Singlet Fission Dimers to a Near-Infrared-Emissive Spin-Flip Emitter,” Journal of the American Chemical Society, March 25, 2026 (DOI: 10.1021/jacs.5c20500) · SciTechDaily, Phys.org, ScienceAlert · India MNRE Solar Deployment Data 2024.</em></p><h3>A message from our Founder</h3><p>Hey, <a href="https://linkedin.com/in/sunilsandhu">Sunil</a> here.
