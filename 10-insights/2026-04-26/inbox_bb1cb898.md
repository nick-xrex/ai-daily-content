## Experts Warn the Quantum Cyber
Threat Is Three Years Away.

<h4><strong>Quantum Cryptography · Q-Day · Timeline Collision · World Quantum Day 2026</strong></h4><h3>Experts Warn the Quantum Cyber<br />Threat Is Three Years Away.<br />Three papers in thirteen months just reduced the qubit count to break your encryption by 200 times. The timelines are colliding. The window is closing.</h3><p>In May 2025, breaking RSA-2048 required an estimated 20 million qubits. By March 2026, three research papers had collapsed that number to under 100,000. The same month, Google published a paper showing ECC-256 the cryptography protecting Bitcoin and Ethereum could fall to fewer than 500,000 qubits in nine minutes. Google has set its own internal migration deadline to 2029. Only 38% of organisations globally are doing anything about it.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*z03U1Ksu8uWKSUo_Fjj4_w.png" /></figure><p><strong>I </strong>study IT in Puducherry. Every networking module I have taken relies on the same foundational assumption: RSA and elliptic curve cryptography are secure because breaking them would require computational resources that no machine, classical or quantum, will have for decades. That assumption has been dissolving in real time across the past thirteen months. Three research papers from Google, a Sydney startup, and the INRIA institute in France have collectively reduced the estimated qubit count needed to break RSA-2048 from 20 million to fewer than 100,000. A fourth paper, published on March 30, 2026, applied similar thinking to ECC-256 the cryptography protecting Bitcoin, Ethereum, and most digital signatures and found it could fall to fewer than 500,000 qubits in nine minutes. The word for what is happening is not “progress.” It is a timeline collision.</p><p>The quantum threat to encryption is not new. Peter Shor published the algorithm that theoretically breaks RSA and elliptic curve cryptography in 1994. The gap between Shor’s algorithm existing and any machine being able to run it at scale was always the question. For decades, the honest answer was: the gap is enormous, probably decades wide, nothing to urgently worry about yet. The three papers published between May 2025 and March 2026 have rewritten that answer. Not because quantum hardware has suddenly leaped forward it hasn’t. But because the algorithmic requirements for breaking encryption have dropped by two orders of magnitude in thirteen months, and hardware is advancing at a pace that is now making the remaining gap feel uncomfortably finite.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/934/1*Cwf7gUla1ZpMATRVa86Gqw.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/904/1*VZxPkQM3EZC2kBRTmjaHQQ.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/909/1*B8wfb98yzNau7c2f4_lXaQ.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/901/1*jmoS5MjTRPAAn1XbLnVB7A.png" /></figure><p>Three papers. Thirteen months. The qubit count to break RSA-2048 went from 20 million to under 100,000. The pattern The Quantum Insider describes is the most important sentence in the entire story: “Each order-of-magnitude reduction took less time than the last.” The improvements are not coming from better hardware. They are coming from better algorithms, better error correction codes, and better compilation. Algorithmic progress is harder to predict and harder to bound than hardware progress. Every time a researcher has said “I don’t see another 10x reduction from here,” someone has found one.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*0PlScxYMwzCyAE86MwWp9Q.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/931/1*zs-H9YcobFb5_uzAuOmYyA.png" /></figure><p>The algorithmic reductions would be less alarming if quantum hardware were still in its early demonstrative phase. It is not. IBM achieved a 10x speedup in classical error correction decoding to under 480 nanoseconds. Google’s Willow chip demonstrated quantum error correction below the surface code threshold the fundamental proof that error-corrected quantum computation is physically achievable in late 2024. Quantinuum’s Helios processor, launched November 2025 with 98 barium-137 trapped ions, achieved 48 fully error-corrected logical qubits using iceberg codes a remarkable encoding efficiency. Microsoft and Atom Computing’s Magne machine is targeting 50 logical qubits operational by early 2027. IBM’s roadmap targets 200 logical qubits with 100 million gates by 2029.</p><p>The gap between “where hardware is” and “what the papers require” is still significant. Breaking RSA-2048 with Gidney’s algorithm requires sustaining approximately 900,000 physical qubits for five days of continuous operation. Breaking it with Iceberg Quantum’s Pinnacle architecture requires approximately 98,000 qubits running for a month. Neither machine exists. But the hardware companies that are building toward these capabilities PsiQuantum, IQM, Oxford Ionics, Diraq are explicitly on roadmaps that project systems at this physical scale within three to five years. Iceberg Quantum noted this explicitly when announcing Pinnacle: they are already working with PsiQuantum, Diraq, IonQ, and Oxford Ionics, several of which have publicly projected these timelines. The algorithmic target and the hardware roadmap are now in the same conversation. That is new.</p><p>Google’s decision to set a 2029 internal PQC migration deadline announced in early 2026, six years ahead of the NIST 2035 mandate carries specific weight because Google’s own researchers are producing the resource estimates that define the threat. When the organisation publishing the papers that shrink the qubit requirements is also the organisation setting an aggressive internal timeline for its own cryptographic migration, the inference is not subtle.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/892/1*safYfhBoYQVwFNkvuTKZxA.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/932/1*S64I5RlCRF-lUyO89g_wVg.png" /></figure><p>The most urgent dimension of the quantum cyber threat is not the day quantum computers can break encryption. It is what is happening right now, years before that day arrives. Nation-state intelligence agencies and sophisticated criminal organisations are conducting what security researchers call “harvest now, decrypt later” operations: collecting encrypted data today government communications, corporate secrets, medical records, financial transactions, legal documents that they cannot read now but will be able to read when quantum computers are available.</p><p>This is not speculation. The NSA, CISA, and NIST have all publicly acknowledged that harvest now decrypt later attacks are already underway. Gary Mounsor, Senior Cybersecurity Consultant at e2e-assure, stated on World Quantum Day 2026: “Our expectation is that a nation state will make inroads with the technology before 2028, after which Pandora’s Box will be open. We can expect to see mass data dumps on the dark web, mass credential abuse, and it declared open season on corporate systems.” Chaim Mazal, CISO at Gigamon, is more blunt: “Attackers are actively harvesting encrypted data with the expectation that quantum computing will make it readable. Despite these concerns, a dangerous assumption persists: 76% of organisations still believe their encrypted data is inherently secure.”</p><p>The implication is concrete and uncomfortable: data encrypted today with RSA or ECC using standards that are not yet broken and will not be broken for years is potentially already in adversarial storage waiting for the decade when quantum machines make it readable. Every encrypted communication from every government agency, every corporate file protected by today’s standards, every financial record, every healthcare database. The harvest is already happening. The decrypt-later is coming.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/933/1*3jxXwMzky163tqEbKVj9sA.png" /></figure><p>Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is not a form of quantum cryptography. It does not use quantum mechanics. It is classical mathematics specifically, algorithms based on mathematical problems that are believed to be hard for quantum computers to solve, unlike the factoring and discrete logarithm problems that RSA and ECC rely on. NIST finalised its first three PQC standards in August 2024: ML-KEM (for key encapsulation), ML-DSA (for digital signatures), and SLH-DSA (for hash-based signatures). A fourth backup algorithm, HQC, was selected in March 2025. The standards are available. The algorithms are published. The migration path exists.</p><p>The problem is migration. Every TLS connection, every encrypted database, every VPN, every digital signature in every system that exists uses the old standards. Replacing all of it is not a software update. It is a multi-year infrastructure project requiring a complete inventory of where cryptography exists in an organisation’s environment, a prioritisation of which systems protect data with the longest required confidentiality windows, a phased migration to PQC algorithms while maintaining compatibility with systems that have not yet migrated, and testing and certification of every replaced component. This is why the Global State of Post-Quantum and Cryptographic Security Trends report shows only 38% of organisations globally are currently transitioning. The other 62% have not started. The US government mandated federal agencies begin transition by 2035. Canada requires federal departments to submit PQC migration plans by April 2026. The EU Cyber Resilience Act is evolving toward a Quantum-Safe-by-Design framework.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*HF6bpccWDSnnWAytu3R26A.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/933/1*YpR9YW5o04J4hPAExSM5QQ.png" /></figure><p>I study networking and systems. In every module, every lab, every assignment the security layer is assumed. We configure TLS. We implement certificate chains. We use RSA keys as a matter of course, the way you plug in a power cable without thinking about the physics of alternating current. The cryptography is infrastructure. It is assumed to work. The conversation happening in quantum research right now is about how long that assumption has left.</p><p>Three papers in thirteen months reduced the qubit count to break the assumption by 200 times. The hardware is advancing to meet the remaining gap. Nation-states are storing encrypted data today for decryption tomorrow. 62% of organisations have not started preparing. And 2026 has been designated the Year of Quantum Security by the FBI, NIST, and CISA the first year this was deemed urgent enough to name.</p><p>I am not a security researcher. I cannot independently evaluate whether the Pinnacle architecture will perform in practice the way it performs in simulation, or whether the Oratomic/Caltech paper’s 26,000-qubit neutral atom estimate will survive peer review. What I can evaluate is the trajectory and the trajectory is not ambiguous. The qubit count is falling. The hardware is rising. The timelines are colliding. The data is already being harvested. The migration has barely started. The gap between when Q-Day arrives and when organisations will have finished preparing is narrowing from both ends simultaneously.</p><p>Every piece of infrastructure I will work on after I graduate the networks, the databases, the encrypted connections, the certificate chains is currently secured by cryptography that a sufficiently powerful quantum machine will be able to break. Whether that machine arrives in 2029 or 2032 or 2035 determines whether the systems I build in my first five years of professional life need to be rebuilt from scratch. This is not an abstract future problem. It is the professional context into which every IT student graduating in the next decade is walking. And right now, only 38% of the organisations we will work for have started dealing with it.</p><p><em>Sources: The Quantum Insider, “Q-Day Just Got Closer: Three Papers in Three Months Are Rewriting the Quantum Threat Timeline” (March 31, 2026) · Craig Gidney, Google Quantum AI, “How to factor 2048 bit RSA integers with less than a million noisy qubits,” arXiv (May 2025) · Iceberg Quantum, “Pinnacle Architecture: RSA-2048 under 100,000 qubits” (February 2026) · Google Quantum AI + Ethereum Foundation, ECC-256 resource estimates whitepaper (March 30, 2026) · EUROCRYPT 2026, INRIA Rennes, ECDLP-256 qubit reduction paper · postquantum.com: “Google Quantum AI Achieves 10x Reduction in Resources to Break Bitcoin’s Cryptography” · IT Pro: “Google just revised its Q-Day timeline” · TechFinitive: “World Quantum Day 2026” · Enterprise Security Tech: “World Quantum Day 2026: Cybersecurity Enters the Post-Quantum Countdown” · 2026 Global State of Post-Quantum and Cryptographic Security Trends report.</em></p><h3>A message from our Founder</h3><p>Hey, <a href="https://linkedin.com/in/sunilsandhu">Sunil</a> here.
