# The Engineers Getting Laid Off First All Have One Thing in Common. It’s Not Their Tech Stack.

<figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*Rhyqw24VRNtSWw69qNGy0g.png" /></figure><h4>After the 2024–2025 layoff wave, a pattern emerged. The engineers who survived weren’t the best coders — they were the ones who could do this one thing.</h4><p>Between 2023 and 2025, the tech industry laid off more than 500,000 engineers.</p><p>Google. Meta. Amazon. Microsoft. Salesforce. Stripe. Spotify. The list reads like a who’s who of the companies people spent years trying to get into. Engineers who had passed grueling interview processes, who had stock options and RSUs and prestigious titles, found themselves updating their LinkedIn profiles and wondering what happened.</p><p>Most of the coverage focused on the macro: AI displacing jobs, overhiring during the pandemic era, interest rates forcing cost discipline. All of that is true. But it misses something that becomes visible when you look at the micro — at which specific engineers got cut and which ones didn’t.</p><p>I’ve spoken with engineers who survived multiple rounds of layoffs at the same company. I’ve spoken with managers who made the calls. I’ve watched teams get restructured and seen who ended up on the other side.</p><p>The pattern is consistent enough to be useful.</p><p>The engineers who got cut weren’t the worst coders. In most cases, they weren’t underperforming at all by traditional metrics. They were shipping features. They were passing code reviews. They were doing the job.</p><p>But they were doing <em>only</em> the job.</p><h3>What “Only the Job” Actually Means</h3><p>Let me be specific, because this distinction matters.</p><p>There’s a version of engineering where you take tickets from the backlog, implement them well, get them reviewed, ship them, and move to the next ticket. The code is clean. The tests pass. The velocity numbers look good. By every traditional measure, this engineer is performing.</p><p>And in a growth market, with unlimited hiring budgets and the mandate to ship features as fast as possible, this engineer is exactly what companies want. They’re reliable. They’re predictable. They’re productive.</p><p>But when the market turns and companies start asking different questions — not “how fast can we ship?” but “what do we actually need?” — this engineer becomes invisible.</p><p>Invisible is dangerous.</p><p>When a manager has to justify every headcount to a VP who’s justifying every headcount to a CFO, the question they’re asking isn’t “who writes the best code?” It’s “who do we lose if we lose this person?” And for the engineer who takes tickets and ships them well, the honest answer is: “We lose someone who ships tickets well. We’ll find another one.”</p><p>That’s not a survivable answer.</p><h3>The One Thing That Survived</h3><p>Every engineer I’ve spoken with who came out of the 2024–2025 layoff wave intact — whether they stayed at their company or landed quickly after a cut — had one thing in common.</p><p>They owned something that wasn’t easily replaceable.</p><p>Not a technology. Technologies are replaceable. Not seniority. Layoffs cut senior engineers at the same rate as junior ones, sometimes faster because of compensation. Not tenure. Plenty of people with 7 years at a company got cut while people with 18 months survived.</p><p>What they owned was <em>context</em>. Specifically: production context.</p><p>They were the person who knew why the payment service behaves strangely under high load on the first of the month. They were the person who had debugged the database connection leak three times and understood its root cause. They were the person the team called at midnight not because they were on the on-call rotation but because they were the only one who could get the system back up quickly.</p><p>That kind of knowledge doesn’t show up in a JIRA velocity report. It doesn’t show up in lines of code shipped. It’s invisible until something breaks — and then it’s the most visible thing in the room.</p><h3>Why Production Depth Is the Moat</h3><p>Stanford’s Digital Economy Study found that software employment for engineers aged 22–25 dropped nearly 20% from its peak in late 2022 through mid-2025. The same study found that employment for engineers aged 35–49 <em>increased</em> by 9% over the same period.</p><p>That’s not a coincidence. That’s the market pricing in production depth.</p><p>Junior engineers — and junior-thinking engineers at any age — are increasingly competing with AI tools that can generate code, write tests, and implement features from specifications. The value proposition of “I can implement this ticket” is under real pressure.</p><p>The value proposition of “I understand why this system breaks and I can fix it when it does” is not under pressure. If anything, it’s increasing. Because as systems get more complex and AI-generated code proliferates, the ability to reason about production behavior — to understand failure modes, to debug under pressure, to make the system survive contact with reality — becomes rarer and more valuable, not less.</p><p>This is the moat. It’s not a technology moat. It’s an operational moat.</p><h3>The Difference in Practice</h3><p>Here’s what this looks like day to day.</p><p><strong>The engineer without production depth</strong> treats production as the place where code goes after it’s done. Their mental model of the system is the system as designed. When something breaks, they look at the code. They run tests. They ask “what did we change?”</p><p><strong>The engineer with production depth</strong> treats production as the real system and everything else as approximations. Their mental model includes not just how the system works but how it fails. When something breaks, they already have hypotheses before they open a dashboard. They know which components are fragile under which conditions. They know which queries slow down at which data volumes. They know which dependencies have reliability issues.</p><p>The second engineer takes 20 minutes to diagnose an incident the first engineer would spend 4 hours on.</p><p>That 4-hour difference, multiplied across every incident, every quarter, is the difference that shows up in a headcount review.</p><h3>The Four Things That Build This Depth</h3><p>I’ve watched engineers develop production intuition deliberately — not by waiting for incidents to happen to them, but by actively building the habits that accumulate it.</p><p><strong>1. Own your incidents end to end.</strong></p><p>Not just mitigation. Root cause. Not just root cause. Prevention. The engineers who develop the fastest are the ones who treat every production issue as a complete learning cycle: what broke, why it broke, what in the architecture made it possible, what would prevent it from breaking the same way again.</p><p>Most engineers fix the immediate problem and move on. The engineers who build real depth write the postmortem even when nobody asked them to. They open a ticket for the architectural fix even when the sprint is already full. They care about the second-order lesson, not just the first-order fix.</p><p><strong>2. Read production metrics when nothing is broken.</strong></p><p>This sounds basic. Almost nobody does it.</p><p>The engineers who can diagnose incidents quickly are the ones who know what normal looks like. They’ve spent time looking at their service’s latency distribution on a Tuesday afternoon when traffic is normal and nothing is on fire. They know what the connection pool looks like at 80% utilization. They know which dashboards tell them something real and which ones are noise.</p><p>When an incident happens, pattern recognition is what separates a 20-minute diagnosis from a 4-hour one. Pattern recognition requires having seen the pattern before.</p><p><strong>3. Understand the failure modes of every dependency.</strong></p><p>For every external service your system calls — databases, caches, queues, third-party APIs, internal microservices — you should be able to answer: what happens if this is unavailable for 30 seconds? What happens if it becomes slow without failing? What happens if it starts returning malformed responses?</p><p>Most engineers can’t answer these questions. They know the happy path. The failure paths are where production depth lives.</p><p><strong>4. Get on the on-call rotation and take it seriously.</strong></p><p>On-call is the fastest school for production thinking that exists. There is no simulation that replicates the cognitive experience of a production incident at 2 AM. The engineers who volunteer for on-call, who treat each page as a learning opportunity rather than an annoyance, who debrief every incident carefully — they compress years of production intuition into months.</p><p>If your team has an on-call rotation and you’ve been avoiding it, reconsider. The short-term cost is some interrupted nights. The long-term benefit is the kind of depth that makes you the person the team can’t afford to lose.</p><h3>The Harder Truth</h3><p>Here’s something that’s uncomfortable to say but true: a lot of engineers who got laid off in the 2024–2025 wave were good engineers by the metrics that were being tracked.</p><p>They shipped features. They wrote clean code. They collaborated well. By every formal measure, they were performing.</p><p>But formal measures miss the thing that actually determines replaceability: whether the organization could function without you.</p><p>This isn’t about job security for its own sake. It’s about the kind of engineer you’re building yourself into. The engineers who build genuine production depth — who understand their systems as living, breathing, failing things rather than as codebases — become the engineers that companies restructure <em>around</em>, not <em>through</em>.</p><p>That’s a different kind of career.
